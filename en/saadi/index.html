Saadian Qur’ans

Dated Qur’ans with a known copyist or patron from the Saadian period: the ones that have come down to us, and the ones that can be browsed online today.

The best-known surviving Saadian Qur’ans are in the Escorial, where they have been since the Spanish seized the library of Sultan Zaydan in 1612 CE. In this period the old Almohad Qur’ans were restored in Marrakesh and put back into use. Five Qur’ans meet the criteria here: four written for the libraries of the sultan’s family, namely the Qur’an of Princess Maryam (967 AH), of Prince Muhammad b. ʿAbd al-Qadir (968 AH), of Sultan ʿAbd Allah al-Ghalib (975 AH) and of Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur (1008 AH), and a fifth written by the bookbinder Ahmad al-Sufyani in Fez (1025 AH). On the edge of the criteria are dated Qur’ans that lack the place or the copyist.

Criteria

A Qur’an enters the first list when three things hold:

  • The date of copying is stated explicitly in its colophon or endowment deed, not inferred from its style.
  • Its owner is known: the copyist, if named, or the person for whom or for whose library it was made.
  • It was demonstrably copied in Morocco under the Saadians.

The Saadians rose in the Sous in 916 AH / 1510 CE, entered Marrakesh in 931 AH / 1525 CE and Fez in 961 AH / 1554 CE; their realm fragmented after the death of al-Mansur in 1012 AH / 1603 CE.

1

The Qur’an made for the library of Princess Maryam, daughter of Muhammad al-Shaykh

1 Shaʿban 967 AH · c. 27 April 1560 CE
In the reign of ʿAbd Allah al-Ghalib
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteness unknownNot digitised
Owner
Written «برسم خزانة», “for the library” of Princess Maryam, daughter of the Saadian Sultan Muhammad al-Shaykh and sister of ʿAbd Allah al-Ghalib
Copyist
Not named
Today
Rabat, National Library, J 656
Format
Stiff brown paper, with the first and last pages of parchment; 17 lines; modern binding

The oldest dated Saadian Qur’an with a known owner that we have found. Its script is a fine Maghribi hand leaning towards mabsūṭ; the vowels are in red, shadda and sukūn sometimes in blue and sometimes in green, the hamza in yellow, and al-Habti’s pausal marks in bright green. The verse separators are gilded rosettes of different shapes for single verses, fifths and tenths.

At the end is a colophon in gold on lapis blue in six lines within a gilded frame, and two gilded pages. Mohamed Abdelhafid Khabta finds its decoration closest among Saadian Qur’ans to that of her brother ʿAbd Allah al-Ghalib’s Qur’an.

The sources available to us do not give its place of copying, and it is not digitised. Khabta published images of it.

2

The Qur’an made for the library of Prince Muhammad b. ʿAbd al-Qadir

Early Ramadan 968 AH · c. May 1561 CE
In the reign of ʿAbd Allah al-Ghalib
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteness unknownNot digitised
Owner
Prince Muhammad b. ʿAbd al-Qadir, grandson of Sultan Muhammad al-Shaykh, vizier of his uncle ʿAbd Allah al-Ghalib and his governor in Meknes, whom his uncle had killed in 975 AH
Copyist
Not named
Today
Rabat, National Library, J 606
Format
Brown paper; vowels in red, shadda and sukūn in blue, hamza in yellow, waṣla in green, and al-Habti’s pausal marks in red

Its colophon is one of the longest in Maghribi Qur’ans: five pages of Maghribi thuluth on a decorated blue ground within gilded frames. It is a rhymed text in praise of the prince, ending with two verses spoken by the Qur’an itself: «أيا ناظراً رقمي وحسن صنائعي، ومستنشقاً عرفي وأذكى بدائعي، لك الله فادعُ للمعلّم إنه إمامٌ له دانت جميع الصنائعِ», “O you who look upon my script and my fine craftsmanship, and breathe my fragrance and my finest wonders: God be with you; pray for the master, for he is an imam to whom all the crafts have bowed.” Then: «وكان الفراغ منها أوائل رمضان... وتسعمائة», “it was finished in early Ramadan... and nine hundred”.

The colophon, as far as it has been published, does not give the place of copying, and the Qur’an is not digitised. Mohammed al-Manuni published the colophon in 1969, and Khabta later reconstructed it in colour from a black-and-white image.

3

The Qur’an made for the library of Sultan ʿAbd Allah al-Ghalib

Early Ramadan 975 AH · c. 28 February – early March 1568 CE
In the reign of ʿAbd Allah al-Ghalib
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesIncompleteNot digitised
Owner
Written for our lord Abu Muhammad ʿAbd Allah, son of the Commander of the Muslims Abu ʿAbd Allah Muhammad al-Shaykh al-Sharif, that is, Sultan ʿAbd Allah al-Ghalib (r. 964–981 AH / 1557–1574 CE)
Copyist
We did not find his name in the catalogue description
Place
Not given in the catalogue description; the Qur’an was written for the sultan’s own library
Today
London, British Library, Or. 1405
Format
400 folios; about 26 × 20 cm; 17 lines; a large, clear Maghribi script; leaves alternating in colour between beige, yellow and pink

A luxury Qur’an, vocalised with red for the vowels, yellow for the hamza and green for the waṣla, shadda and sukūn. Its margins mark every division of the Qur’an, from halves, thirds and quarters down to tenths, the sixty ḥizbs, and twenty-seven sections for the nights of Ramadan. The sura headings are in gilded Kufic.

Its colophon, in an ornamental white script on a blue ground, runs over four pages (397v–399r), followed by two pages of geometric decoration in gold and colours. The first twelve leaves are modern replacements for those lost at the beginning.

We found no published digitised copy. It is described in Rieu’s catalogue of the supplement of Arabic manuscripts in the British Museum (1894), and Bongianino and Cellard drew attention to its pink leaves.

The Qur’an made for the library of Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur

Wednesday 13 Rabiʿ II 1008 AH · c. 2 November 1599 CE
In the reign of Ahmad al-Mansur
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteFully digitised
Owner
Written «برسم الخزانة العلية الكريمة النبوية الحسنية الإمامية الأحمدية المنصورية», “for the exalted, noble, prophetic, Hasanid, imamic, Ahmadi, Mansuri library”, that is, the library of Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur al-Dhahabi (r. 986–1012 AH / 1578–1603 CE)
Copyist
We did not find his name in the colophon
Place
«بجامع الديوان الكريم من قصور الإمامة العلية», “in the mosque of the noble Diwan in the palaces of the exalted imamate”, in Marrakesh
Today
El Escorial, Royal Library, árabe 1340
Format
Paper; 266 folios; 263 × 177 mm; 17 lines
Reading
Qalun from Nafiʿ

It is known as the Qur’an of Mawlay Zaydan, because it was in the library of al-Mansur’s son, Sultan Zaydan, when the Spanish seized it in 1612 CE, and entered the Escorial with the rest of his books.

Its colophon is a full page in gold on a lapis blue ground, and describes the ink: «بالمداد المقام من فائق العنبر المتعاهد السقيا بالعبير...», “with ink made of the finest ambergris, steeped again and again in perfume...”. François Déroche and Nuria Martínez de Castilla see a clear Ottoman influence in its binding and decoration. Between its lines, small gold ṣāds mark the places of pause set out by Muhammad al-Habti (d. 930 AH).

...برسم الخزانة العلية الكريمة النبوية الحسنية الإمامية الأحمدية المنصورية... ووافق تمامه يوم الأربعاء الثالث عشر من ربيع الثاني عام ثمانية وألف بجامع الديوان الكريم من قصور الإمامة العلية...

Our reading from the image of the folio. The text is long, so we have kept only the passages naming the library and the date. 13 Rabiʿ II 1008 AH corresponds to about 2 November 1599 CE.

5

The Qur’an of Abu al-ʿAbbas Ahmad b. Muhammad al-Sufyani

1025 AH · 1616 CE
In the reign of Zaydan and his brothers
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteness unknownNot digitised
Copyist
Abu al-ʿAbbas Ahmad b. Muhammad al-Sufyani, the bookbinder of Fez, who wrote it in his own hand
Place
Fez
Today
Fez, Qarawiyyin Library, no. 3
Vocalisation
Vowels in red only, hamza in yellow, waṣla in green, and al-Habti’s pausal marks in the colour of the ink; sura openings in Kufic

Al-Sufyani was one of the great bookbinders of the late Saadian period. Four years after copying this Qur’an he wrote his treatise Sinaʿat tasfir al-kutub wa-hall al-dhahab (The art of bookbinding and the dissolving of gold, 1029 AH), one of the most important works on the craft of the book in Morocco. This Qur’an is thus in the hand of a man who was both copyist and binder. In it al-Sufyani departs from the custom of his time and does not use blue for shadda and sukūn.

Not digitised, and Khabta did not publish the full text of its colophon.

In the reign of Muhammad al-Shaykh al-Saghir

Copyist
Ahmad b. ʿAli b. Abi Ibrahim
Written in the name of
Abu Muhammad Sidi ʿAbd Allah al-Ghazwani, the Jazuli saint buried in Marrakesh (d. 935 AH / 1529 CE), so it was most likely endowed to his zawiya
Place
Marrakesh
Today
Doha, Museum of Islamic Art, MS.11.1999: the thirtieth and last volume of a rabʿa in thirty volumes
Format

A rabʿa written in Marrakesh at the end of the Saadian period in the name of ʿAbd Allah al-Ghazwani, one of the Seven Saints of Marrakesh and the head of the Jazuli order in his time. Rabʿas like this were endowed to zawiyas and madrasas so that their volumes could be read. Its colophon is in thuluth, and its date is written in ghubār numerals. The museum classes it as Alaouite, but its date comes about ten years before the Alaouites entered Marrakesh.

One volume out of thirty; the museum publishes an image of two of its pages.

The Qarawiyyin Qur’an no. 5

Before 1020 AH · before 1611 CE
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteness unknownNot digitised
Today
Fez, Qarawiyyin Library ms. 5, volumes 2–5 of six
Format
Coloured paper: yellow, blue, white, red and pink; the text in black, and the name of God and the sura openings in gold
Endowment
To the Qarawiyyin Library in Jumada II 1020 AH, received by its keeper Masʿud Qassara

One of the few Qur’ans written on coloured paper, pink among it. Bongianino and Cellard consider it Saadian; Mohamed Abdelhafid Khabta considers it Marinid. It was in the Saadian period that the Almohad Pink Qur’an was restored in Marrakesh, with leaves added in a hand imitating its original script.

It was copied before 1020 AH / 1611 CE, and the sources available to us give neither its exact date nor its copyist.

The two Qur’ans of Ahmad b. Muhammad b. Husayn

Safar 1015 AH and Rabiʿ II 1016 AH · 1606 CE and 1607 CE
In the reign of Zaydan and his brothers
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteNot digitised
Copyist
Ahmad b. Muhammad b. Husayn, who copied both
Dates
A Wednesday in Safar 1015 AH (árabe 1873); and a Friday in the last ten days of Rabiʿ II 1016 AH, about 17 August 1607 CE (árabe 1257)
Today
El Escorial, Royal Library, árabe 1257 and árabe 1873

The two are close in script and page layout, and each has more than fifty different designs for the ḥizb and prostration ornaments, in many colours. They were probably copies for personal use from the circle of Sultan Zaydan.

Their origin is disputed: Déroche and Martínez de Castilla think they come from the Iberian Peninsula, while Arias Torres thinks they are Moroccan, because their markers for ḥizbs 15 and 22 follow the new Maghribi practice.

Arias Torres, the Escorial Qur’ans (2025)

The Qur’an of the captive Muhammad b. ʿAbd al-Wahid al-Tazi

Thursday 16 Muharram 1065 AH · 26 November 1654 CE
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesIncompleteFully digitised
Copyist
Muhammad b. ʿAbd al-Wahid b. ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Tazi, “the Maghribi of Fez”, according to the colophon
Place
The island of Malta, where he was a captive of the Knights of St John
Today
Philadelphia, University of Pennsylvania, Kislak Center, Ms. Codex 1904
Format
Paper; 238 folios; 87 × 86 mm; 15 lines; the first leaf missing

A small Qur’an in Maghribi script, written by a captive from Fez in Malta, who calls himself in the colophon “the captive who confesses his sin and shortcoming”. Its sura headings are in gold on yellow, and at the end is a colophon in a red and yellow frame.

In a study of 2024, Luca Berardi argues that its copyist is Muhammad al-Tazi, captured in 1651 CE, who later converted to Christianity and became a Jesuit under the name Baldassarre Loyola Mandes (1631–1667 CE); European sources call him a prince of Fez.

Its date and copyist are explicit, but it was copied in Malta, not in the Saadian lands.

The colophon (fol. 238r)

كملت الختمة المباركة بحمد الله تعالى وحسن عونه على يد الأسير المعترف بالذنب والتقصير، الراجي رحمة مولاه [...]، محمد بن عبد الواحد بن عبد القادر التازي المغربي الفاسي، الأسير [...] دمّرها الله. وكان الفراغ منه يوم الخميس بعد صلاة الصبح [...] بجزيرة مالطة دمّرها الله، يوم ستة عشر في شهر محرم عام خمسة وستين وألف. ورحم الله الكاتب والكاسب والقارئ والمستمع...

It was finished on Thursday after the dawn prayer [...] on the island of Malta, may God destroy it, on the sixteenth of the month of Muharram of the year one thousand and sixty-five. May God have mercy on the writer, the buyer, the reader and the listener...

Our reading from the image; what is in square brackets is not legible in it. 16 Muharram 1065 AH corresponds to 26 November 1654 CE, as in the library’s catalogue.

The second half of a Qur’an in the hand of Saʿid b. ʿAli al-Darʿi

3 Dhu l-Qaʿda 1069 AH · 23 July 1659 CE
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesIncompleteFully digitised
Copyist
Saʿid b. ʿAli b. Zakariyya b. Masʿud b. ʿUmar, from the Draa, according to the description of the Bibliothèque nationale de France
Today
Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France (Gallica)
Format
174 folios: prayers, then the second half of the Qur’an (Maryam – al-Nas), then prayers

Its copyist finished it before sunrise, according to the library’s description, in the year the Saadian state ended with the killing of its last sultan in Marrakesh.

Its date and copyist are explicit, but the place of copying is not stated, and the Draa was then outside Saadian rule.

Limits of this guide

We relied on what has been published and could be consulted. The Royal Library (al-Khizana al-Hasaniyya) in Rabat and the Qarawiyyin Library most likely hold dated Saadian Qur’ans that have neither a published catalogue nor a digitised copy. The book Les livres du sultan (2022) includes a catalogue of the dated Saadian manuscripts in the Escorial, whose full text we could not reach.

Some images are reproduced from published studies, mostly at low resolution, to introduce Qur’ans that have not been digitised; the source of each image is given under it, and the rights belong to their owners and to the libraries that hold the manuscripts.
