Almohad Qur’ans

Dated Qur’ans with a known copyist or patron from the Almohad period in the Maghrib and al-Andalus: the ones that have come down to us, and the ones that can be browsed online today.

The Almohad state spanned both shores of the Strait, so its Qur’ans were copied in Marrakesh and Ceuta as well as in Seville, Valencia and Malaga. Few of them meet the criteria here; the best known is the multi-volume Qur’an that the Caliph al-Murtada wrote in his own hand. On the edge of the criteria stand many Qur’ans that are dated or endowed but do not name their copyist, or that were copied in cities temporarily outside Almohad rule.

Criteria

A Qur’an enters the first list when three things hold:

  • The date of copying is stated explicitly in its colophon or endowment deed, not inferred from its style.
  • Its owner is known: the copyist, if named, or the person for whom or for whose library it was made.
  • It was demonstrably copied in the Almohad lands of the Maghrib and al-Andalus, while they were under Almohad rule.

Some Andalusian cities broke away from Almohad rule during this period: Valencia under Ibn Mardanish until 567 AH / 1172 CE, and Seville under Ibn Hud around 626–635 AH / 1229–1238 CE. Qur’ans copied there at those times are placed on the edge of the criteria.

Jumada II 599 AH · c. 14 February – 14 March 1203 CE
In the reign of al-Nasir
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteness unknownNot digitised
Copyist
Muhammad b. Muhammad al-Sharishi
Illuminator
His son-in-law Yusuf the illuminator; his father’s name has not been read in the colophon
Made for
Prince Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf, son and heir of the Caliph al-Nasir (r. 595–610 AH)
Place
Marrakesh
Today
Istanbul, Topkapı Palace Library, R. 33
Format
A single small oblong volume; 22.3 × 18 cm

A princely Qur’an whose colophon says it was made to celebrate the birth of Prince Abu Yaʿqub, son and heir of the Caliph al-Nasir, who succeeded his father and took the title al-Mustansir. The dedication is in Maghribi thuluth reserved on a ground of gold leaf, and its wording, “among what [the copyist] made... for [the prince]”, puts the copyist’s name before the prince’s. A copyist and an illuminator worked on it together, as in the Almoravid Qur’an in Munich.

Not digitised. Martin Lings published two of its pages in his book on Qur’anic calligraphy and illumination (1976); the images here are from Bongianino’s thesis (2017).

5

The rabʿa of the Caliph al-Murtada, in his own hand

20 Jumada II – 6 Rajab 654 AH · July 1256 CE
In the reign of al-Murtada
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesIncompleteNot digitised
Owner and copyist
The Almohad Caliph Abu Hafs ʿUmar al-Murtada (r. 646–665 AH / 1248–1266 CE). The colophon of volume 2 reads «وكتبه بخط يده الفانية», “written by his own mortal hand”.
Place
Marrakesh, according to the colophon
Endowment
To the Saqaya mosque, i.e. the mosque of ʿAli b. Yusuf in Marrakesh, in Rajab 656 AH / July 1258 CE, “for all Muslims, night and day”. The notarial endowment deed is in volumes 1 and 4, in zimām script, and below it the endowment is confirmed in al-Murtada’s own hand.
Today
Marrakesh, Ibn Yusuf Library ms. 432: four volumes according to Bongianino, five according to Khabta, among them volume 1 (432/1) and volume 4 (432/4); Rabat, National Library J 658 (volume 2) and J 1278; Oudaya Museum, Rabat (one or two volumes once exhibited there); London, British Library Or. 13192 (a fragment of volume 7 in its original binding, bought at Sotheby’s in 1969).
Format
Parchment; 72 folios in volume 2; nine lines to the page; 290 × 225 mm; a bold mabsūṭ in black ink, some of whose letter forms lean towards the Andalusi script.
Vocalisation
Vowels and madda in red, sukūn and waṣla in dark green, hamza in orange-yellow.
What survives
Volumes of a set of ten, each of six ḥizbs.

The oldest surviving Qur’an of the Islamic West written by a ruler in his own hand. Volume 1 is dated 20 Jumada II 654 AH and volume 3 6 Rajab of the same year: sixteen days separate the completion of the first from that of the third.

The verse separators are gilded roundels divided into triangles by fine black lines. The markers of every fifth and tenth verse are gilded roundels, some topped by a pyramid-like form. The sura headings are in coloured Kufic within rectangular frames decorated with gold inlaid with red and blue, joined to ornaments in the margin. In the basmala the kashīda of «الرحمن» stretches between the ḥāʾ and the mīm in the Andalusi manner. Al-Murtada sometimes splits a word between the end of one line and the start of the next, as in «أبصـ/ـارهم» (al-Baqara 20), following the habit of early Qur’ans that al-Qalqashandi criticised.

The colophon of volume 1 is in Maghribi thuluth, its letters gilded and outlined in black, with a touch of eastern thuluth. Ibn ʿIdhari reports that al-Murtada wrote three scripts, and that after his deposition in 665 AH he settled on Jabal Saksawa near Marrakesh and “lived by copying”.

The Qur’an remained complete in the Ibn Yusuf Library in Marrakesh until 1149 AH (1736–1737 CE), when the city’s muḥtasib borrowed it; its volumes were then dispersed. Volume 2 in Rabat was only identified when Mohammed al-Manuni described it and assigned it to the set, comparing its script with that of the other volumes and noting that its date agrees with theirs.

No volume has been digitised for the public. Two pages were published in the exhibition Le Maroc médiéval at the Louvre in 2014.

The Qur’ans of the Banu Ghattus in Valencia

556–564 AH · 1160–1169 CE
In Valencia under Ibn Mardanish
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteness unknownNot digitised
Copyists
The family of Ibn Ghattus, the famous copyists of Valencia; the Egyptian National Library holds a Qur’an signed by Ibn Ghattus the father and dated 557 AH / 1161–1162 CE
Owner of the 557 AH Qur’an
The vizier Abu Muhammad ʿAbd Allah b. ʿAbd al-Rahman b. ʿAbd Allah al-Madhhiji al-Lushi, whose name comes before the copyist’s in the colophon
Place
Valencia
Number
Four Qur’ans on parchment between 556 and 564 AH

Among the oldest known dated and signed Qur’ans of al-Andalus. Their colophons were in Maghribi thuluth, and Ibn Ghattus the son was said to have copied a thousand Qur’ans. His Qur’an dated 578 AH was copied after Valencia came under Almohad rule, so it is listed in the first group.

Dated and with known copyists, but Valencia was then ruled by Ibn Mardanish, independent of the Almohads, and only came under their rule in 567 AH / 1172 CE.

Today

One of the few surviving dated Andalusi Qur’ans, copied in Seville, the Almohad capital in al-Andalus, shortly before the Almohads lost it. It is complete in one volume, and its colophon is written in Kufic within a gilded roundel (shamsa).

The colophon in the gilded roundel (folios 264–265 of the digitised copy)

كمل المصحف المبارك بعون الله وحسن عونه، وصلى الله على سيدنا محمد وعلى آله وسلّم تسليماً، وكان الفراغ منه في العشر الأول من شهر المحرم عام أربعة وعشرين وستمائة بمدينة إشبيلية حرسها الله.

Before 22 Shaʿban 635 AH (date of its endowment) · before 9 March 1238 CE
Page of this Qur’an →

Endowment
Endowed by Prince Abu Ishaq Ibrahim, al-Murtada’s brother, in Marrakesh on 22 Shaʿban 635 AH, by four identical deeds in his own hand in the margins of the four volumes
Today
Istanbul, Topkapı Palace Library, R. 21–24
Format
Paper; four volumes; leaves of 57.5 × 49.5 cm, among the largest Qur’ans copied in the Islamic West

Bongianino transcribed the endowment deed, in which Abu Ishaq entrusted its supervision to the jurist Abu Muhammad ʿAbd al-Wahid b. Makhluf. The Marinid Sultan Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf then re-endowed it in Fez on 20 Rabiʿ I 696 AH and had a colophon added in gold at the end of volume 4.

The Qur’an itself is undated and names no copyist. It was probably written by one of the two calligraphers of the Pink Qur’an.

The Tinmal rabʿa

632 AH (copied) · 649 AH (endowed) · 1235 CE · 1251 CE
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesIncompleteNot digitised
Place
Seville
Endowment
Endowed by al-Murtada to the tomb of the Mahdi Ibn Tumart at Tinmal on Friday 16 Ramadan 649 AH
Today
Marrakesh, Ibn Yusuf Library ms. 429
What survives
Volume 16 of a set of twenty

The only surviving object that can be linked with certainty to the shrine of Tinmal. The endowment deed stands at the head of the volume, marked with the Almohad motto «والحمد لله وحده», “Praise be to God alone”.

It was copied in Seville in 632 AH, when the city was ruled by Ibn Hud, who had rebelled against the Almohads. It is therefore not an Almohad work, and became one only through its endowment. The sources do not name its copyist.

Endowed by the caliph al-Murtada in Safar 650 AH to the tomb of one of his wives, “the mother of our son Ishaq”, most likely in Marrakesh, under the supervision of two preachers
Place
Most likely Marrakesh
Today
Marrakesh, Ibn Yusuf Library ms. 431, which holds eight of its ten volumes (vol. IV and X are missing); scattered leaves in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (2017.232), the J. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles (Ms. 122), the Museum of Islamic Art, Doha (MS.806.2011), and elsewhere
Format
Paper dyed pink; ten volumes, as its endowment deed states; five lines to the page
Name
Also known as the “Xàtiva Qur’an”, because its paper was described as “Xàtiva paper” (al-waraq al-Shatibi) in the handlist of the 1960 Qarawiyyin exhibition and then by al-Manuni in 1969, as Bongianino and Cellard report. They find no evidence that its paper was imported from Xàtiva: fine paper was also made in Seville, and in their view pink paper was most likely made in the Maghrib as well, particularly in Marrakesh. The Metropolitan Museum and the Getty still attribute it to al-Andalus

A large-script Qur’an on pink paper, most likely copied in Marrakesh in the second quarter of the thirteenth century CE. Two calligraphers worked on it, and it was illuminated in two phases or by two illuminators. It used to be attributed to al-Andalus, until Bongianino and Cellard (2025) showed it to be an Almohad, Maghribi work.

In the upper margin of every original leaf, needle holes spell the word hubs (“endowment”). They were made in the Marinid period, at the very end of the thirteenth or in the early fourteenth century CE, when the Marinids took the Qur’ans endowed by the Almohads and re-endowed them. It was later restored and endowed again under the Saadians and the Alaouites.

Undated, and no copyist is named. Its latest possible date comes from its endowment deed.

Anatomy of the script

How to read the script of the Pink Qur’an

Five lines to the page in a monumental mabsūṭ script on paper dyed pink. This is a detail of a leaf at the Metropolitan Museum, from Surat Yunus (48–49): «إن كنتم صادقين. قل لا أملك لنفسي ضرا ولا نفعا إلا ما شاء...», “...if you are truthful. Say: I have no power to harm or benefit myself, except as God wills...”. Its vocalisation differs from that of the Marinid Qur’ans in ways the anatomy shows.

Swipe the image sideways to see the full lines.

  1. 1
    Verse number in abjad lettersIn the gilded verse roundel are two red letters, «مح»: mīm is 40 and ḥāʾ is 8, so this is verse 48 of Surat Yunus.
  2. 2
    Vowels in goldThe fatḥa, kasra and ḍamma are gilded strokes outlined in black here, not red as in the Marinid Qur’ans we have seen.
  3. 3
    Shadda in blueThe shadda is drawn in blue above the letter, as on the lām of «إلّا».
  4. 4
    Sukūn as a blue circleA small blue circle above the vowelless fāʾ of «لنفْسي».
  5. 5
    Hamza as a yellow dotAn orange-yellow dot on the top of the alif of «أملك» marks the hamza.
  6. 6
    Sweeping curvesThe end of «قل» drops below the line in a wide curve, as throughout Maghribi mabsūṭ.
  7. 7
    The pink paperThe paper was dyed pink before writing, which is rare in Qur’ans. It was most likely made in Marrakesh itself in the last decades of the Almohad state.

Limits of this guide

We relied on what has been published and could be consulted. The Ibn Yusuf Library in Marrakesh, the National Library and the Royal Library (al-Khizana al-Hasaniyya) in Rabat, and the Qarawiyyin Library hold other Almohad Qur’ans that have neither a detailed published catalogue nor a digitised copy. We did not find the names of the copyists of the Malaga and Tinmal rabʿas; they may appear in printed works we could not reach, such as the catalogue of the exhibition Masahif al-Maghrib (2011).

The thesis of Mohamed Abdelhafid Khabta (2011) mentions a Qur’an in the Vatican Library (Vat. ar. 214) that it says “bears the date 597 AH”, in the reign of al-Nasir. We did not include it: the library’s description makes it an undated fragment of the seventh century AH (al-Ahzab 31 – Fatir 45) taken from Tunis in 1535 CE, and Bongianino does not list it among the dated Maghribi Qur’ans.
