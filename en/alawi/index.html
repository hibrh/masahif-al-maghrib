Alaouite Qur’ans

Dated Qur’ans with a known copyist or patron from the Alaouite period, from Mawlay al-Rashid to the present day: manuscript and printed, and the ones recorded in the sources.

The Alaouite period is the longest and the closest to us, and it continues today. Its manuscript Qur’ans are plentiful in Moroccan libraries, but few have been studied and described in print: the Qur’an of Prince ʿAli in Cairo, the Qur’ans of a rural woman copyist in the Royal Library, and the rabʿa of al-Qandusi. Then lithography reached Fez, where the first Qur’an was printed in 1296 AH, and in the modern era Qur’ans came to be written by named calligraphers and printed by royal order: the Zouitn Qur’an, al-Mushaf al-Hasani, al-Mushaf al-Hasani al-Musabbaʿ and al-Mushaf al-Muhammadi.

Criteria

A Qur’an enters the first list when three things hold:

  • The date of copying is stated explicitly in its colophon or endowment deed, not inferred from its style.
  • Its owner is known: the copyist, if named, or the person for whom or for whose library it was made.
  • It was demonstrably copied in Morocco under the Alaouites.

The Alaouite period began when Mawlay al-Rashid entered Fez in 1076 AH / 1666 CE. Some colophons do not give the place of copying, so we count a Qur’an as Moroccan when its copyist or the person it was written for lived in Morocco and nothing points elsewhere. This guide is about manuscript Qur’ans, so we placed printed Qur’ans at the edge of the criteria, and included those whose calligrapher and date of printing are known, since printing in Morocco went on reproducing the hand of a particular calligrapher.

1

The Qur’an of the Alaouite Prince ʿAli

1142 AH · 1729–1730 CE
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteness unknownNot digitised
Owner
Written for the Alaouite Prince ʿAli, grandson of Sultan Mawlay Ismaʿil, according to al-Manuni; Khabta says it was written for the library of Sultan ʿAbd Allah b. Ismaʿil
Copyist
Not named in the sources available to us
Today
Cairo, Egyptian National Library (Dar al-Kutub), Masahif 25
Format
Maghribi script with Andalusi features, adorned and decorated in gold and colours; the last sura headings in gilded panels, and coloured rosettes in the margin
Later endowment
At the end, in naskh, a note that it was endowed by «محمد بيك بجامعه», “Muhammad Bey to his mosque”, perhaps the emir Muhammad Bey Abu al-Dhahab, founder of the mosque that bears his name in Cairo (1188 AH)

It counts among the treasures of the Egyptian National Library and is described in its catalogue (vol. 1, p. 2). It is the oldest dated Alaouite Qur’an with a known owner that we found in published sources. Mohamed Abdelhafid Khabta published an image of its last two pages and three of its illuminated openings. In 1142 AH ʿAbd Allah b. Ismaʿil was sultan, so the Qur’an may have been for his library, as Khabta says, or for a prince of his family, as al-Manuni says; we have not seen the text of its colophon to decide between them.

Not digitised for the public, as far as we know.

Al-Manuni’s article (1968, Arabic)

The Qur’ans of the woman copyist ʿAʾisha bint Mubarak al-Takiyya

1237 AH and 1245 AH · 1822 CE and 1830 CE
In the reigns of Mawlay Sulayman and Mawlay ʿAbd al-Rahman
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteness unknownNot digitised
Copyist
ʿAʾisha bint al-Hajj Mubarak al-Shalh al-Takki, who in one of the Qur’ans calls herself ʿAʾisha bint Mubarak b. Ahmad, son of al-Hasan al-Shaykh, al-Takki al-Ghashi al-Hasnawi
First Qur’an
Rabat, Royal Library, no. 4087: the morning of Friday 22 Jumada [I or II] 1237 AH / 1822 CE
Second Qur’an
Rabat, Royal Library, no. 5061: Sunday 22 Dhu l-Qaʿda 1245 AH / c. 16 May 1830 CE
Third Qur’an
Rabat, Royal Library, no. 4225: in the margin at its end, in a different ink, “year 1237”
Script
A clear, medium-sized badawī (rural) hand, fully vocalised in colour, as al-Manuni describes it

A rare witness to a Moroccan woman who copied Qur’ans and wrote her name and the date of copying in their colophons. Mohammed al-Manuni made her known, and mentions with her the sharīfa Fatima bint ʿAli al-Zabadi of Fez, who copied more than thirty-five Qur’ans.

Al-Manuni does not give the place of copying; the copyist came from the Moroccan countryside.

Al-Manuni’s article (1968, Arabic)
3

The rabʿa of Muhammad b. Abi al-Qasim al-Qandusi

Friday, end of Shawwal 1266 AH · c. 6 September 1850 CE
In the reign of Mawlay ʿAbd al-Rahman
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteness unknownNot digitised
Copyist
Muhammad b. Abi al-Qasim al-Qandusi of Fez (d. 1278 AH / 1861 CE)
Made for
al-Hajj Idris b. al-Wazir Muhammad b. Idris al-ʿAmrawi of Fez
Today
Meknes, al-Khizana al-Zaydaniyya, no. 3595 in the new catalogue
Format
A large rabʿa in 12 volumes of five ḥizbs each, in a magnificent hand

Al-Qandusi was one of the most famous calligraphers of nineteenth-century Fez, with a style of Maghribi script that bears his name. The author of Salwat al-anfas writes in his biography: “He had a fine, excellent hand, in which he wrote a number of copies of the Dalaʾil, and I was told that he wrote a Qur’an in twelve volumes whose like is rarely found in the world.”

He wrote it for al-Hajj Idris al-ʿAmrawi, who led Morocco’s embassy to France in 1860 CE and wrote its account; it later passed to the Zaydaniyya library in Meknes.

Omar Afa and Mohamed al-Maghrawi published in their book al-Khatt al-maghribi: tarikh wa-waqiʿ wa-afaq two pages of a Qur’an in al-Qandusi’s hand kept in the Royal Library in Rabat: the start of ḥizb 21 (al-Tawba 93) and the start of ḥizb 56 (the opening of Surat al-Jumuʿa), each topped by a gilded band with the basmala and the blessing on the Prophet, as volumes open. These two ḥizbs open volumes 5 and 12 of a rabʿa with five ḥizbs to the volume, which matches al-ʿAmrawi’s rabʿa, so they most likely come from it.

Mohamed Sijelmassi published in his book on the manuscripts of the Royal Library (1987) a detail of a “noble Qur’an” in al-Qandusi’s hand kept there as no. 12613 (al-Bayyina 6–7). We found nothing to show that the Royal Library manuscript is this same rabʿa, passed there from the Zaydaniyya.

Not digitised for the public, as far as we know.

Al-Manuni’s article (1968, Arabic)

5

The first Qur’an lithographed in Fez

1296 AH · 1878–1879 CE
In the reign of al-Hasan I
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteNot digitised
Printer
The press of al-Hajj al-Tayyib b. Muhammad al-Azraq of Fez
Place
Fez

The first Qur’an printed in Morocco, some fifteen years after lithography reached Fez. Lithography reproduces the copyist’s own hand, so a Qur’an printed this way is an image of a manuscript, which is why Moroccans preferred it to movable type.

Printed, not manuscript, and we have not found the name of the copyist whose hand it reproduces.

Al-Manuni’s article (1968, Arabic)

The Habbabi Qur’an in the hand of Ahmad b. al-Husayn Zouitn

1347 AH (first printing) · 1928–1929 CE
In the reign of Muhammad V
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteNot digitised
Calligrapher
The copyist (warrāq) Ahmad b. al-Husayn Zouitn of Fez; some sources give his father’s name as al-Hasan
Printed through the care of
al-Hajj Muhammad al-Mahdi al-Habbabi
Correction
Three masters of the Qur’anic readings in Morocco
Printing
Lithographed in Egypt in 1347 AH / 1928–1929 CE, then reprinted many times, among them by the Moroccan Ministry of Habous

The best-known Moroccan printed Qur’an of the twentieth century. Zouitn wrote it in mabsūṭ script and al-Habbabi had it lithographed, so the print reproduced the calligrapher’s hand as it was. It spread through Moroccan households, and the Ministry of Habous reprinted it for the beauty of its mabsūṭ hand.

Printed, not manuscript. We included it because its original is in the hand of a known calligrapher and the date of its first printing is certain.

The article “al-Kitaba al-mushafiyya bi-l-Maghrib” on Maʿlama (Arabic)Interview with the researcher Ahmed Saidi (2013, Arabic)
7

Al-Mushaf al-Hasani in the hand of Ahmad al-Bahawi al-Susi

Written c. 1390 AH, printed 1400 AH · c. 1970 CE and 1980 CE
In the reign of al-Hasan II
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteNot digitised
Calligrapher
Ahmad b. al-Husayn al-Bahawi al-Susi (d. 28 May 2000 CE), brother of the calligrapher Muhammad al-Bahawi, author of the Taʿlim al-khatt copybooks printed in Spain in 1949. Al-Manuni called him Muhammad by mistake, as Omar Afa and Mohamed al-Maghrawi point out
Ordered by
King Hassan II
Printing
Ministry of Habous and Islamic Affairs, 1400 AH / 1980 CE, according to Afa and al-Maghrawi. The description of an exhibition of the Rabita Muhammadiyya of ʿUlama mentions two earlier editions, in 1387 AH / 1967 CE and 1395 AH / 1975 CE

Omar Afa and Mohamed al-Maghrawi write: «ومن الأعمال الخالدة التي عرفتها هذه المرحلة إنجاز المصحف الحسني بأمر ملكي سام في عهد المغفور له الملك الحسن الثاني، أشرفت على طبعه وزارة الأوقاف والشؤون الإسلامية سنة 1400هـ / 1980م», “Among the lasting works of this period was the making of al-Mushaf al-Hasani by royal order in the reign of the late King Hassan II, printed under the supervision of the Ministry of Habous and Islamic Affairs in 1400 AH / 1980 CE”, and they say that al-Bahawi «نال شرف كتابة المصحف الحسني في بداية السبعينيات», “had the honour of writing al-Mushaf al-Hasani in the early seventies”. He wrote it in mabsūṭ script, and it made the name «المصحف الحسني», al-Mushaf al-Hasani, well known. When it was reprinted in 1417 AH, the royal order stated that the new edition «تمتاز على الطبعات السابقة بتسبيعها», “excels the earlier editions by its division into sevenths”, so the Musabbaʿ Qur’an below is a new edition of al-Mushaf al-Hasani in other hands.

Printed, not manuscript. We included it because its calligrapher is known and the date of its printing is certain.

Interview with the researcher Ahmed Saidi (2013, Arabic)

Al-Mushaf al-Hasani al-Musabbaʿ, the Hasani Qur’an in sevenths

1417 AH · 1996–1997 CE
In the reign of al-Hasan II
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteFully digitised
Calligraphers
Seven Moroccan calligraphers, one for each seventh, in the order of their hands in the Qur’an: Mohamed Maalmine, Mahmoud Amzal, Abdelilah Amzal, Mohamed Masslouhi, Imam Ouardighi, Jamal Bensaid and Mohamed Laith
Decoration
Abdellah Ouazzani
Ordered by
King Hassan II, through an order to the Minister of Habous and Islamic Affairs, Abdelkebir Alaoui M’daghri
Reading
Warsh from Nafiʿ

It opens with a royal order that reads: «أصدرنا أمرنا المنيف إلى وزيرنا في الأوقاف والشؤون الإسلامية الفقيه عبد الكبير العلوي المدغري بإعادة طبع المصحف الحسني الشريف طبعة جديدة فاخرة أنيقة تتوفر لها شروط الصحة والضبط والإتقان، وتمتاز على الطبعات السابقة بتسبيعها على المأثور في قراءة السلك وختمها، وتميز الأسباع بخطها وزخرفتها», “We have issued Our order to Our Minister of Habous and Islamic Affairs, the jurist Abdelkebir Alaoui M’daghri, to reprint the noble Hasani Qur’an in a new, fine and elegant edition meeting the conditions of correctness, vocalisation and precision, excelling the earlier editions by its division into sevenths, following tradition in the reading and completion of the silk, with the sevenths distinguished by their script and decoration.” So each seventh has its own calligrapher and decoration, after the Moroccan custom of reading the Qur’an in seven sevenths.

Printed, not manuscript. We included it because its calligraphers are known and the date of its printing is certain.

Calligrapher
Belaid Hamidi of Fez. He taught calligraphy at the Royal College in the Royal Palace in Rabat (1992–2008), then at al-Azhar University in Cairo (2008–2016), and has lived in Jakarta since 2018
Qur’ans
Eight Qur’ans in Maghribi script since 1999, according to his biography on the CALLFRONT project website

One of the leading Moroccan Qur’an calligraphers today, combining the Maghribi script he grew up with in Fez and the eastern scripts in which he was licensed in Istanbul. We found no published description of each of his Qur’ans with its date and patron, so we have grouped them in one card.

10

Al-Mushaf al-Muhammadi

1431 AH · 2010 CE
In the reign of Muhammad VI
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesCompleteFully digitised
Calligrapher
Mohamed Maalmine, in mabsūṭ script, with the sura titles in Maghribi thuluth
Decoration
Abdellah Ouazzani
Ordered by
King Mohammed VI, by royal order no. 1.09.199 of 8 Rabiʿ I 1431 AH (23 February 2010 CE)
Review
The committee of supervision, review and correction, chaired by Abdelhadi Hamitou
Printing
The Mohammed VI Foundation for the Publication of the Holy Qur’an; legal deposit no. 2010 Mo 2880

King Mohammed VI ordered «بالعمل على تخطيط القرآن العظيم في مصحف شريف يحمل اسم المصحف المحمدي», “the writing of the Glorious Qur’an in a noble muṣḥaf bearing the name al-Mushaf al-Muhammadi”, and Mohamed Maalmine wrote it at the request of the Ministry of Habous. The review committee attested at its end that it is written and vocalised «برواية ورش عن نافع من طريق أبي يعقوب الأزرق», “in the reading of Warsh from Nafiʿ by way of Abu Yaʿqub al-Azraq”. The Mohammed VI Foundation for the Publication of the Holy Qur’an prints about a million copies a year for the mosques of Morocco, the mosques of Moroccans abroad and the countries that read in the Warsh tradition.

Printed, not manuscript. We included it because its calligrapher is known and the date of the order for it is certain.

Limits of this guide

For the older Qur’ans we relied mainly on Mohammed al-Manuni’s article “Markaz al-mushaf al-sharif bi-l-Maghrib” (1968); for the modern ones, on what the Ministry of Habous and Islamic Affairs has published, on Moroccan press and websites, and on the Qur’ans themselves where we found scanned copies. The Royal Library, the National Library, the Qarawiyyin Library and the libraries of the zawiyas hold many dated Alaouite Qur’ans, most of them with no published catalogue and no digitised copy. Western libraries hold Qur’ans in Maghribi script dated to this period that name neither copyist nor place, such as a volume in the Bavarian State Library dated 1273 AH (Cod.arab. 2850) and a small Qur’an at Princeton University dated Rabiʿ I 1212 AH (Garrett 50G); we did not include them. In the modern era, calligraphers have written complete Qur’ans for which we found no published description with a date, among them al-Hajj ʿUthman Jourio under the Protectorate, whose copy is in the library of the Great Mosque of Rabat, Mohamed Ali Atfay (five Qur’ans between 1984 and 1992), and ʿAbd al-Rahim Koulin (2005 and 2009).
