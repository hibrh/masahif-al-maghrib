Marinid Qur’ans

Dated Qur’ans with a known copyist or patron from the Marinid period: the ones that have come down to us, the ones that are lost, and the ones that can be browsed online today.

Gathered here are the Qur’ans that published catalogues and studies show were written in the Marinid state, whose colophons or endowment deeds give a specific date and the name of the person who wrote them or for whom they were written. A separate section lists well-known Marinid Qur’ans that do not meet the criteria, so that the reason for leaving them out is clear.

Overview

What do we know?

This guide lists 14 Qur’ans in three groups: those that survive and meet the criteria, those recorded in the sources and since lost, and those on the edge of the criteria.

Criteria

A Qur’an enters the first list when three things hold:

  • The date of copying is stated explicitly in its colophon or endowment deed, not inferred from its style.
  • Its owner is known: the copyist, if named, or the person for whom or for whose library it was made.
  • It was demonstrably copied in the Marinid state, or for one of its people.

The Qur’an made for the library of Sultan Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf

Last ten days of Rajab 705 AH · c. 6–15 February 1306 CE
In the reign of Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesIncompleteFully digitised
Owner
Written «برسم خزانة», “for the library” of Sultan Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf b. Yaʿqub (r. 685–706 AH / 1286–1307 CE), as the colophon states
Copyist
Not named in the colophon
Place
Marinid Morocco (the colophon does not name the city)
Today
Munich, Bavarian State Library: Cod.arab. 2 (first volume) and Cod.arab. 3 (last volume)
Format
Parchment; 26 × 21.5 cm; seven lines to the page
What survives
Two of twelve volumes: the first (al-Fatiha – Al ʿImran 14), 122 folios, and the last (al-Jumuʿa – al-Nas), 115 folios

One of the most splendid surviving Maghribi Qur’ans. It was in twelve volumes of five ḥizbs each, of which only the first and the last survive. At the end of the last volume is a colophon in gold in a decorated frame, giving the date of completion and stating that it was written for the sultan’s library.

The text is in Maghribi mabsūṭ in black ink, seven lines to the page, with the vocalisation in colour. The sura headings are in gilded Kufic, each with a round ornament in the margin beside it. The phrase «لا إله إلا هو», “there is no god but He”, is written in gold wherever it occurs in the text, and the Qur’an has gilded opening and closing pages.

The colophon (end of the last volume, fol. 121v)

كمل المصحف المبارك بيمن الله تعالى وعونه، والصلاة على محمد نبيّه ورسوله وعلى آله وصحبه، وذلك في العشر الأُخَر من شهر رجب الفرد عام خمسة وسبع مائة، برسم خزانة مولانا أمير المسلمين أبي يعقوب يوسف ابن أمير المسلمين أبي يوسف بن عبد الحق، خلّد الله بالعز أيامه، ونصر بالفتح أعلامه.

The blessed Qur’an was completed by the grace and help of God Almighty, with blessings on Muhammad, His Prophet and Messenger, and on his family and companions, in the last ten days of the unique month of Rajab of the year seven hundred and five, for the library of our lord the Commander of the Muslims Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf, son of the Commander of the Muslims Abu Yusuf b. ʿAbd al-Haqq, may God make his days endure in glory and give victory to his banners.

Our reading from the image of the folio. “The last ten days” are the final ten days of Rajab 705 AH, about 6–15 February 1306 CE. «برسم خزانة», “for the library”, means that it was written to be kept in the sultan’s library.

3

The Maghribi rabʿa: the Qur’an of Abu al-Hasan in his own hand

745 AH · 1344–1345 CE
In the reign of Abu al-Hasan
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesIncompleteNot digitised
Owner and copyist
Sultan Abu al-Hasan ʿAli b. ʿUthman (r. 731–752 AH / 1331–1351 CE), who copied it himself
Endowment
To al-Aqsa Mosque, to be read in the Dome of the Rock
Today
Jerusalem, Islamic Museum of al-Aqsa Mosque (box no. 0152)
Format
Parchment; square pages of five lines; a broad, bold Maghribi mabsūṭ in dark brown ink
Vocalisation
Vowels, madda and elision marks in red; sukūn, shadda and waṣla in blue; hamza in yellow
What survives
25 of 30 volumes in the sultan’s hand, 24 of them in the museum since volume 30 was stolen

It is called “the Maghribi rabʿa” because its thirty volumes are kept in a square box of ebony inlaid with silver, divided inside into compartments. The text is in Maghribi mabsūṭ, in a carbon ink mixed with saffron and musk. The sura headings and endings are in plain, foliated and interlaced Kufic, and at the end of each volume is the endowment text in seven lines of gilded Kufic within a gilded frame.

Volumes 5, 10, 16, 18 and 26 were lost long ago and were replaced in 1221 AH (1806 CE) in the hand of a Maghribi, al-Hajj Mubarak b. ʿAbd al-Rahman, called “al-Maliki” in some sources and “al-Mashunshi” by Khabta. Volume 30 (from Surat al-Nabaʾ to Surat al-Nas) was stolen from the museum in 1931–1932, most likely taken to Egypt. The founder stipulated that it be read every day after sunrise, by a reader who knew the Qur’an by heart and was of good character.

The verse separators are gilded roundels with the word «آية», “verse”, in their centre, ringed by three circles with small red and green circles alternating between them, on the model of the separators in the rabʿa of his father Abu Saʿid. Fifth and tenth verses, ḥizbs and the divisions for Ramadan have gilded markers in the margin, each of its own shape. The sura headings are in Kufic on a blue foliate ground, or in Maghribi thuluth on a ground of gilded spiral scrolls, and the volumes end with «صدق الله العظيم ورسوله الكريم», “God Almighty and His noble Messenger have spoken the truth”, in Maghribi thuluth in a gilded frame.

Ibn Marzuq records in his Musnad that Abu al-Hasan copied other Qur’ans in his own hand: for the ribāṭ of Chella, for the Prophet’s Mosque and for the Sacred Mosque. The al-Aqsa rabʿa is the only one of them that survives.

Not digitised for the public. Its images are published in Khader Salameh’s catalogue of the Qur’ans in the Islamic Museum (2001) and in an illustrated report by Al Jazeera Net (2020). The two images here are reproduced from Dr Mohamed Abdelhafid Khabta’s course, at low resolution.

A leaf from a Qur’an written in gold

Undated · c. 1250–1300 CE according to the museum
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesIncompleteFully digitised
Today
New York, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, 37.21 (acquired in 1937)
Format
A single parchment leaf; 26 × 21.7 cm; 9 lines of Maghribi mabsūṭ, its letters in gold outlined in ink
Text
Surat al-Nisaʾ 72–76
Origin
“North Africa” according to the museum record

A rare leaf whose letters are written in gold and vocalised in colour: vowels in red, sukūn as blue circles, shadda in blue, waṣla as a green dot and hamza as a yellow dot. The verse separators are gilded rosettes with lapis blue at their centre.

Mohamed Abdelhafid Khabta’s thesis (2011, p. 188, fig. 43) reproduces a page from Surat Yunus (61–64) in the same style, captioned “two pages of a Qur’an written in liquid gold in 700 AH in the reign of Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf the Marinid”, with a reference to the Metropolitan as 37.21; many Arabic websites repeat it. But the museum’s leaf 37.21 is from Surat al-Nisaʾ, not Surat Yunus, so the Yunus page is another leaf whose whereabouts we have not found. The museum dates leaf 37.21 to about 1250–1300 CE, mentions neither the year 700 AH nor Abu Yaʿqub, and the leaf has no colophon.

Undated, and neither its copyist nor its owner is known. The date of 700 AH and the attribution to Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf have no support in the museum record, whose estimate could fit the end of the Almohad period as well as the beginning of the Marinid.

Today
Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, Arabe 385

A complete Qur’an in one volume, written in the year Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf sent his rabʿa to Mecca. Its opening and closing pages are a geometric lattice around an eight-pointed star, drawn in white lines on a ground of gilded arabesque. The verse separators are three gilded dots.

The date is explicit and the Qur’an complete, but its colophon names neither copyist nor owner. Its place of copying is uncertain: it may come from Nasrid Granada rather than Marinid Morocco. We include it for comparison.

The colophon in gilded Kufic (folios 130v–131r)

كمل المصحف بحمد الله وحسن عونه، وصلى الله على سيدنا ونبينا ومولانا محمد وعلى آله الطيبين الطاهرين، وسلّم أفضل التسليم، والحمد لله رب العالمين. وكان الفراغ منه في آخر شهر جمادى الأخرة عام ثلاثة وسبعمائة.

The Qur’an was completed with the praise of God and His good aid; may God bless our lord, our Prophet and our master Muhammad and his good and pure family, and grant them the most excellent peace; praise be to God, Lord of the worlds. It was finished at the end of the month of Jumada II of the year seven hundred and three.

Our reading from the images of the two folios; the image here is of the second, which has the date. The colophon gives no copyist, no owner and no place of copying.

The Qur’an made for the library of Sultan Abu Saʿid ʿUthman

Between 710 and 731 AH · 1310–1331 CE
In the reign of Abu Saʿid
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesIncompleteNot digitised
Owner
Written for the library of Sultan Abu Saʿid ʿUthman, according to the colophons of volumes 1 and 16
Today
Cairo, al-Azhar Library (volume 1, 64 folios); Rabat, National Library K 2949 (four folios of volume 16 with its colophon)
Format
Parchment; originally in 30 volumes

It was probably written and illuminated in Fez. It later left the royal library and was endowed to an unknown mosque, and the margins of its leaves were pricked with a needle to spell the word «حبس», “endowed”: a Marinid way of recording an endowment that is harder to erase than a note in ink.

Its two colophons name the owner of the library but are undated, so its date is known only within the span of his reign.

Volume 7 of a rabʿa endowed by Abu ʿInan

Between 749 and 759 AH · 1348–1358 CE
In the reign of Abu ʿInan
Page of this Qur’an →
SurvivesIncompleteFully digitised
Owner
It bears an endowment note attributed to Sultan Abu ʿInan Faris, no longer fully legible
Today
Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, Arabe 423
Format
Parchment; 197 × 181 mm; nine lines; 114 folios
What survives
Volume 7 of eight (from the opening of Surat Sad to al-Tur 49)

The clearest digitised example of how Marinid Qur’ans were vocalised in colour: vowels in red, shadda and sukūn in blue, hamza as an orange dot and waṣla as a green dot. Every fifth verse is marked by a gilded Kufic hāʾ, with the word «خمس», “five”, in the margin; every tenth by a roundel with the word «عشر», “ten”, in gold on a red ground. At the beginning and end are geometric panels centred on an eight-pointed star.

The Qur’an is undated and names no copyist. It is attributed to the reign of Abu ʿInan only because of its endowment.

The rabʿa of Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf for the Sacred Mosque

1304 CE · In the reign of Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf
Lost

Ibn Khaldun writes: “Yusuf ordered a finely made Qur’an to be copied; it was written and embellished by Ahmad b. al-Hasan the accomplished scribe. He made it large, had its cover made with exquisite craft, and gave it many gold clasps set with pearls and rubies... and endowed it to the Holy Sanctuary.” It was sent with the pilgrim caravan in 703 AH. Ibn Marzuq saw it in Mecca after its ornaments had been stripped, and wrote: “I saw in Mecca the Qur’an that our lord Abu Yaʿqub sent, in the hand of Ibn Hasan... It remained in the dome of the mihrab, read for the sake of God, and I read in it for several years.” No trace of it is known today.

Anatomy of the script

How to read the Marinid script

Three lines of Surat al-Baqara (75), close up, in volume 1 of the Qur’an of Abu Yaʿqub Yusuf (705 AH): «لكم وقد كان فريق منهم يسمعون كلام الله ثم يحرفونه من بعد ما», “...you, when a party of them heard the word of God and then distorted it after...”. Click the numbers or the list to see the features of the script and its vocalisation.

Swipe the image sideways to see the full lines.

  1. 1
    Shadda in blueThe shadda is drawn in blue above the letter, as on the mīm of «مِّنهم». You can also see it on the lām of «الله» and the mīm of «ثمّ».
  2. 2
    Sukūn as a blue circleThe sukūn is a small blue circle above the vowelless letter, as on the ʿayn of «بعْد».
  3. 3
    Waṣla as a green dotA green dot on the top of the alif marks the elided hamza (hamzat al-waṣl), as on the alif of «الله».
  4. 4
    Vowels in redThe fatḥa, ḍamma and kasra are small red marks: the letters are written in black ink and the vocalisation in a different colour that stands apart from them.
  5. 5
    The Maghribi kāfThe kāf of «لكم» is written with a long upper stroke stretched over what follows, one of the hallmarks of Maghribi script.
  6. 6
    Sweeping curvesIn mabsūṭ the tails of nūn, yāʾ and mīm drop below the line in wide curves, like the nūn of «يسمعون». This is the most visible trait of the script.
  7. 7
    Qur’anic orthography«كلم الله» is written without an alif, following the ʿUthmanic orthography that Qur’ans keep.

Limits of this guide

We relied on what has been published and could be consulted among catalogues and studies. The Qarawiyyin Library in Fez, the National Library and the Royal Library (al-Khizana al-Hasaniyya) in Rabat, and the Ibn Yusuf Library in Marrakesh hold many Marinid Qur’ans that have neither a detailed published catalogue nor a digitised copy. The list is therefore the documented minimum, not everything that was written. One example is a volume of a rabʿa endowed to a shrine at al-Qulla outside Fez (Qarawiyyin Library, no. 1966, with volume 1, no. 1981), whose date of copying we could not find. Khabta takes it to be a Marinid rabʿa of thirty volumes on gazelle parchment, endowed in the Saadian period by Masʿuda, the mother of al-Mansur.

Khabta’s thesis (2011) also attributes to this period several Qur’ans that we left out because we could not verify them or found otherwise: two Qur’ans he says were copied in 648 AH, one in the Bibliothèque nationale de France with an unclear reference and the other sold at Sam Fogg in London, whose dates we found nothing to confirm; and a Qur’an written in silver on paper, dated around 807 AH (Arabe 389), which he took to be Marinid from the reign of Abu Saʿid ʿUthman, but which the Bibliothèque nationale de France attributes to the Hafsid Sultan Abu Faris ʿAbd al-ʿAziz al-Mutawakkil b. Ahmad, so it is Tunisian.
